# The 26 Humanizer patterns in English

Editorial reference for Humanizer ChatGPT English 1.0, based on the Humanizer ChatGPT PT-BR adaptation and Humanizer 3.1.0 from the supplied archive. All examples in this reference are illustrative. Their details are premises of the examples, not verified claims about real products or services.

## Contents

- A. Staging before substance: patterns 1 to 5.
- B. Formulaic rhythm: patterns 6 to 11.
- C. Inflation and borrowed authority: patterns 12 to 18.
- D. Automatic formatting: patterns 19 to 21.
- E. Leftovers from the conversation and draft: patterns 22 to 25.
- F. The reader's context: pattern 26.
- Exceptions, final check, and a complete example.

## How to interpret the signals

Patterns 1 to 5 warrant a closer look even in a single occurrence when their only purpose is to stage importance. Patterns 8, 9, 10, 11, and 21 are weak in isolation; a single description of the document's organization under pattern 25 is also weak. Consider what the construction does, how often it appears, the genre, and the writer's voice.

No word, construction, or publication date proves that a text was written by AI. The aim is to improve the reading experience. An explicit style preference can justify keeping a construction listed here. Preserve meaning, conditions, facts, quotations, and uncertainty throughout the edit. Keep the text's language unless the user requests translation; use the grammatical conventions of that language.

## A. Staging before substance

### 1. Automatic contrast: "not X, but Y"

**Watch for:** "not just," "more than," "it's not about X, but Y," "X rather than Y" without a real comparison, or the same contrast split across sentences.

**Edit:** state the point directly when the negative half introduces a position nobody held. Keep contrasts that correct an actual misunderstanding or express two distinct pieces of information.

**Before:** "This form isn't just a screen. It offers a new way to organize registrations."

**After:** "This form offers a new way to organize registrations."

**Keep when needed:** "The payment was authorized, but it has not yet been captured." Both states matter.

### 2. Dramatic closers and fragments

**Watch for:** "That's the real win," "Let that sink in," "Simple as that," a standalone sentence that repeats the preceding paragraph, or sequences such as "No waiting. No doubt. No limits."

**Edit:** remove the repeated closer. Combine fragments when a complete sentence expresses the content more clearly. Keep short sentences that add a fact, a consequence, or deliberate emphasis.

**Before:** "The cache avoids repeating the query. That's the real win."

**After:** "The cache avoids repeating the query."

### 3. Statements that imitate depth

**Watch for:** "at its core," "what really matters," "the real question," "the architecture of trust," or an abstract metaphor used in place of an explanation.

**Edit:** replace the formula with the specific claim. Keep metaphors that explain something or belong to the writer's voice.

**Before:** "At its core, what really matters is whether the team can keep providing support."

**After:** "The question is whether the team can keep providing support."

### 4. Staged introductions before the point

**Watch for:** "let's dive in," "without further ado," "here's what you need to know," "to be completely honest," "here's the thing," or a staged question and answer before an ordinary statement.

**Edit:** start with the information. An occasional conversational phrase can be natural; the problem is announcing every point before making it.

**Before:** "Let's look at how this works. Access ends after 24 hours."

**After:** "Access ends after 24 hours."

### 5. Answering objections nobody raised

**Watch for:** "I'm not saying," "don't get me wrong," "some might think," or an invented alternative introduced only to be rejected.

**Edit:** remove the empty defense and retain any substantive claim. Keep the reader's actual objections and plausible alternatives in a requested comparison.

**Before:** "I'm not saying other methods are useless. For this form, validating the required fields is enough."

**After:** "For this form, validating the required fields is enough."

## B. Formulaic rhythm

### 6. Forced triads

**Watch for:** groups of three near-synonyms, three examples with the same structure, or three sentences followed by a predictable lesson.

**Edit:** use as many items as the subject requires. Merge redundancies or vary the structure without discarding distinct items, sequence, ranking, or simultaneous actions.

**Before:** "The tool helps you organize, arrange, and put your files in order."

**After:** "The tool helps you organize your files."

**Keep when needed:** "We accept bank transfers, cards, and cash." The three payment options are different pieces of information.

### 7. Repeated sentence openings

**Watch for:** several sentences starting with "he," "she," "the system," "additionally," or the same construction without a deliberate effect.

**Edit:** combine sentences or vary their organization. Keep purposeful repetition in literature, speeches, or the writer's voice sample.

**Before:** "She opened the file. She checked its name. She saved the copy."

**After:** "She opened the file, checked its name, and saved the copy."

### 8. Dashes used for everything

**Watch for:** dashes around every explanation, qualification, or conclusion. A single dash is a weak signal.

**Edit:** when dashes serve as generic connectors, consider periods, commas, colons, or parentheses. If the writer supplies a sample, match its use of dashes where appropriate.

**Before:** "The update—available today—includes a filter—requested by users."

**After:** "The update available today includes a filter requested by users."

**Keep:** meaningful punctuation in dialogue, exact quotations, numerical ranges, mathematical notation, and occurrences in code, commands, paths, and URLs. Do not confuse a hyphen with a dash or remove a minus sign as a style edit.

### 9. Stacked qualifications

**Watch for:** "could potentially perhaps," "might possibly be able to," or "it is possible that in some cases it may." A qualification on its own is normal.

**Edit:** keep the uncertainty that matters and remove only redundant layers. Preserve scope, conditions, corrections, relevant notices, and limits of the evidence.

**Before:** "The change could potentially perhaps reduce waiting times."

**After:** "The change could reduce waiting times."

**Do not turn it into:** "The change reduces waiting times." That would change a possibility into a result.

### 10. Mechanical hyphenation and awkward compounds

**Watch for:** compound modifiers hyphenated in every grammatical position, unnecessary coined compounds, or labels that make a simple idea harder to understand.

**Edit:** in English, consider the compound's grammatical role, dictionary spelling, and the requested style guide. Many compound modifiers take a hyphen before a noun and are open after it, but this is not a universal rule. Keep established spellings, technical terms, names, and identifiers. For text in another language, follow that language's conventions rather than importing the English rule.

**Before:** "The report is high-quality."

**After:** "The report is high quality."

**Keep when needed:** "a high-quality report," dictionary spellings such as "well-being," command-line options, and proper names. Do not remove a correct hyphen merely to make the text look less formal.

### 11. Passive voice and missing subjects

**Watch for:** actions with unclear responsibility or repeated passive constructions. Either can be a weak signal in isolation.

**Edit:** use active voice if the actor is known and naming that actor improves clarity. Do not invent who performed an action. Keep passive voice when the result matters more than the actor. Supply a missing subject when English syntax and the context require one; keep appropriate fragments in headings, labels, or an established voice. Do not import another language's rules for omitting subjects.

**Before:** "The system stores the records. The records are deleted by the system after 30 days."

**After:** "The system stores the records and deletes them after 30 days."

**Keep when needed:** "The file was removed." If the responsible person or process is unknown, do not assign the removal to someone.

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

### 12. Vocabulary repeated by habit

**Watch for:** clusters of words such as "crucial," "robust," "transformative," "dynamic," "ecosystem," "landscape," "leverage," "enhance," "delve," and "tapestry" when they add no precision.

**Edit:** prefer familiar, specific words. Do not build a universal list of forbidden vocabulary. Keep legitimate technical uses, such as "robust statistics," and the writer's expressive choices.

**Before:** "The tool facilitates organizing files into folders."

**After:** "The tool helps organize files into folders."

### 13. Inflated importance and promised futures

**Watch for:** "a historic milestone," "a pivotal moment," "a lasting legacy," generic challenges-and-outlook sections, or endings that promise a bright future without supporting information.

**Edit:** keep the event, its effects supported by the material, and actual plans. Remove grand language that adds no content. End on the last concrete point when the conclusion only repeats enthusiasm.

**Before:** "The library opened in 1998, marking a historic moment in the neighborhood's journey."

**After:** "The library opened in 1998."

### 14. Vague relationships between people or things

**Watch for:** "linked to," "associated with," "in connection with," or "involved with" when the text already identifies the relationship.

**Edit:** name the relationship supplied by the source or writer. If it is unknown, retain the uncertainty or ask for clarification.

**Before:** "Marina is associated with the organization, which she founded and chairs."

**After:** "Marina founded and chairs the organization."

**Do not infer:** that someone is a director merely because they are "associated with the company."

### 15. Empty interpretations added with -ing phrases

**Watch for:** "highlighting," "reinforcing," "symbolizing," "demonstrating," "fostering," or "ensuring" tacked onto a sentence without support for the claimed effect.

**Edit:** keep actions and consequences supported by the material. Remove only the empty commentary. An -ing form is not itself a problem; the unsupported interpretation is.

**Before:** "The team published the schedule, demonstrating its unwavering commitment to excellence."

**After:** "The team published the schedule."

**Keep when needed:** "She entered the room carrying two boxes." The simultaneous actions matter.

### 16. Generic advertising language

**Watch for:** "an unparalleled experience," "a must-visit destination," "revolutionary," "breathtaking," "nestled in the heart of," and vague benefits.

**Edit:** in factual writing, describe what exists. In requested advertising copy, keep the persuasive purpose and call to action through concrete benefits. Do not invent differentiators, scarcity, free access, or guarantees.

**Before:** "Discover an unparalleled organizing experience: the app lets you group tasks by project."

**After:** "Group your tasks by project in the app."

### 17. Borrowed authority

**Watch for:** "experts say," "research proves" without an identifiable reference, or lists of publications and follower counts used as substitutes for evidence.

**Edit:** use the source and what it actually said when those details are in the material. Do not invent a source or remove concrete facts, metrics, or publications just to simplify a list. A specific claim does not become false merely because it lacks a citation. Preserve who made the claim: a report recording someone else's opinion is not necessarily endorsing it.

**Before:** "Experts say, according to Team Alpha's evaluation report, that the timeline is achievable."

**After:** "According to Team Alpha's evaluation report, experts say the timeline is achievable."

**If no source is given:** remove only an empty appeal when doing so preserves the claim and its certainty; if confidence in the claim depends on the attribution, flag the missing support. Do not turn "experts say it works" into "it works" as a verified fact.

### 18. Avoiding "is," "are," and "has"

**Watch for:** "serves as," "constitutes," "stands as," "boasts," or "features" when a simple verb would express the same meaning.

**Edit:** use the direct verb without changing function, condition, or scope. "May serve as a shelter" does not mean "is a shelter."

**Before:** "The room serves as the team's headquarters and features two desks."

**After:** "The room is the team's headquarters and has two desks."

## D. Automatic formatting

### 19. Decorative bold and repeated labels

**Watch for:** several highlighted words in every sentence or lists whose labels merely repeat their explanations.

**Edit:** remove emphasis that serves no purpose. Use prose when it makes reading easier and lists when items need to be found or compared. Preserve every distinct detail and any necessary hierarchy.

**Before:** "**Deadline:** The deadline is Friday. **Delivery:** Delivery will be by email."

**After:** "The deadline is Friday, and delivery will be by email."

**Keep:** important notices, field names in instructions, and emphasis requested by the user.

### 20. Decorative headings and symbols

**Watch for:** automatic title capitalization, stock emojis, purposeless arrows, dividers between every paragraph, or a title repeated immediately below itself.

**Edit:** write headings that identify their content and use capitalization suited to the language and requested style. In English, title case can be legitimate; use sentence case when it fits the document rather than treating title case as proof of anything. Preserve proper names, informative symbols, and formatting required by the genre or user.

**Before:** "🚀 Strategic Publishing Plan"

**After:** "Strategic publishing plan"

**Keep:** an arrow showing an actual sequence or an emoji that belongs to the supplied voice sample.

### 21. Quotation marks that do not fit the format

**Watch for:** inconsistent mixing of straight and curly quotation marks or punctuation that does not suit the destination. Curly quotes are not evidence of AI authorship.

**Edit:** standardize only when the format or preference calls for it. Both curly and straight quotes can work in prose. In code, preserve the characters required by the syntax.

**Example:** in text requiring straight quotes, change `“revision”` to `"revision"`. Do not alter the content of a verbatim quotation or code delimiters to satisfy an aesthetic preference.

## E. Leftovers from the conversation and draft

### 22. Chatbot wrappers

**Watch for:** "Great question!", "Certainly!", "Here is", "I hope this helps", or "Would you like me to continue?" inside text that needs to stand on its own.

**Edit:** remove the wrapper and keep the content. Preserve greetings, thanks, and sign-offs that actually belong to a letter, message, or customer interaction.

**Before:** "Sure! Here's the notice: customer support closes at 6 p.m. I hope this helps!"

**After:** "Customer support closes at 6 p.m."

### 23. Knowledge limits and guesses in place of information

**Watch for:** generic mentions of the model's training, a lack of sources followed by a speculative explanation, or guesses about unavailable personal details.

**Edit:** preserve genuine limits on the information and remove unsupported guesses. Do not delete reference dates, research scope, scientific qualifications, or relevant warnings to make the text sound more confident.

**Before:** "The document does not give the founding date; the company probably began in the 1990s."

**After:** "The document does not give the company's founding date."

**Keep when needed:** "Prices checked on November 6, 2026." The date defines the information's scope.

### 24. An opening sentence that repeats the heading

**Watch for:** a heading such as "Registration deadline" followed by "Knowing the deadline is important" before the useful information.

**Edit:** remove the repetition without deleting new content. Do not confuse repetition with a necessary definition.

**Before:** "Registration deadline. Knowing the deadline is important. Registration closes on Friday."

**After:** "Registration deadline. Registration closes on Friday."

### 25. Writing about how the document was assembled

**Watch for:** "the table below presents," "this document was compiled," or "this section is organized by" when the reader can already see that. An isolated occurrence is a weak signal.

**Edit:** write about the subject. Preserve methodology needed for interpretation, attributions, data provenance, non-obvious conventions, and explanations of changes in change reports or migration guides.

**Before:** "The table below lists the deadlines. Orders ship in two business days."

**After:** "Orders ship in two business days."

**Keep when needed:** "The sample includes only active customers." That changes how the results should be read.

## F. The reader's context

### 26. Re-explaining what the reader already knows

**Watch for:** a reply that repeats the problem and the entire investigation before reaching the decision, even though the conversation shows that the reader already knows the situation.

**Edit:** lead with the decision and keep the new reasons that support it. Match the length to the request. If the user asks for a full report, preserve the necessary evidence and explanation. Without enough context, do not assume the reader knows the subject.

**Example context:** the conversation has already established that the form has a bug and that the fix is ready.

**Before:** "As we discussed, the form has a bug and the fix is ready. My proposal is therefore to publish the fix at 3 p.m."

**After:** "I propose publishing the fix at 3 p.m."

## Exceptions that apply to all 26 patterns

- Preserve quotations, names, official titles, and passages discussing an expression rather than using it.
- Prioritize the explicit request, audience, and voice sample. A deliberate construction may work well.
- Preserve opinions, doubts, humor, and experiences already in the original. Do not manufacture personality.
- Keep every relevant fact and limitation. Naturalness does not justify changing a claim's strength or discarding inconvenient details.
- Protect code, placeholders, commands, paths, URLs, formulas, data, and metadata. Apply the edit only to the authorized prose.
- Do not classify authorship or promise a result from an AI detector.

## Final check

Compare the original and revision for information, negations, conditions, sources, numbers, names, dates, units, exceptions, certainty, sequence, rankings, and simultaneous actions. Check that reorganizing lists has preserved distinct items. Remove only redundancy and empty formulas unless the user has authorized summarizing or adapting the content.

## Complete example

**Illustrative original:**

"We're thrilled to announce a truly transformative update! This new registration dashboard isn't just another screen; it's a new way to organize registrations. Starting Monday, the new registration dashboard may be used on the Basic and Pro plans at no additional cost. It shows received registrations and lets you filter them by date. Simple as that."

**Revision:**

"Starting Monday, the new registration dashboard may be used on the Basic and Pro plans at no additional cost. It shows received registrations and lets you filter them by date."

**Check:** the revision retains the relative date, the new dashboard, both plans, the absence of an additional charge, the availability wording, and both functions. It removes generic enthusiasm, an empty contrast, and a repeated closer. It adds no statistics, testimonials, or benefits absent from the original.
